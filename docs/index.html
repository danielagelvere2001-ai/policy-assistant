Company Policy Assistant: three approaches compared

9 test questions against a database of 98 company policies · model gemini-3.6-flash, embeddings gemini-embedding-001 · Daniela Gelvere

Rules-based searchKeyword matching with synonyms and IDF weighting. Returns the best policy's text word for word, with no LLM.
LLM without vector indexAll 98 policies are pasted into the Gemini prompt for every question.
LLM with vector indexPolicies are embedded once. Each question retrieves the top 3 most similar policies, and only those go to Gemini.

Discussion

On the nine test questions, the rules-based search was by far the fastest (under 1 ms) and used no tokens, but it named the right policy for only 4 of 9 questions. Because it always returns the closest keyword match, it answered all three questions the database does not cover with an unrelated policy: dental insurance became the product Warranty Policy, bringing a dog became the badge-focused Office Access Policy, and the guest Wi-Fi password became the employee Password Policy. It also missed two paraphrased questions ("work from home" and "working from Spain"), so 5 of its 9 answers were unsupported by the policy database. Both LLM approaches found the correct policy 9 of 9 times, gave no unsupported answers, refused all three out-of-scope questions, and said explicitly when a policy lacked the detail asked for (for example the number of sick days accrued per month) instead of inventing a number. The difference between them is cost: the LLM without a vector index sends all 98 policies with every question (about 2,850 tokens and a median of 12.6 s), while the LLM with a vector index sends only the three most similar policies (about 320 tokens, roughly 9 times fewer, and a median of 2.0 s).

The preferred approach is the LLM with a vector index, and it is the one the Slack bot uses. It matched the full-context LLM's accuracy and groundedness at about a tenth of the tokens and with faster answers. It also scales: the full-context prompt grows with every policy added and would eventually hit context limits and cost, while the retrieval step keeps each prompt small. Its main risk is that the right policy might not be among the retrieved ones; in this test the correct policy was always ranked first (similarity 0.71–0.73), and for out-of-scope questions the LLM correctly ignored the weaker matches. These results come from a small sample (nine questions, limited by the Gemini free tier), one model (gemini-3.6-flash), and response times that include varying network delays, so they show a clear pattern rather than precise figures. The rules-based search remains useful as a free, instant fallback when no LLM is available.

How it was measured

  • Correct policy: the policy the approach named is one of the acceptable policies for that question. For out-of-scope questions the only correct result is “no policy”.
  • Unsupported answer: every answer was reviewed against the full policy database after the run (by Claude, an AI assistant, because the Gemini free tier allows only ~20 requests per model per day). An answer counts as unsupported if it states anything the database does not say, or presents an unrelated policy as the answer. The same rules are implemented as an automatic Gemini judge in evaluation/run_eval.py for runs with a larger quota.
  • Response time: wall-clock time of one call from a laptop, including the network round trips to the Gemini API (the query embedding plus generation for the vector approach).
  • Tokens: reported by the Gemini API (prompt + output). The vector approach also counts its query-embedding tokens.
  • Runs: Questions 1, 5 and 6 were run on 6 Oct 2026 and the rest on 9 Oct 2026 (free-tier daily limits). Network latency to the free Gemini tier varies a lot, so the median time is the fairer comparison.
